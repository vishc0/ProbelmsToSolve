# Opportunity: Universal Knowledge, AI, and Internet Access

> Open access measurement and offline knowledge with local AI help communities compare paths to affordable basic digital access.

## Owner direction

`[human-direction]` Treat knowledge, AI, and internet access as basic necessities that should be free or subsidized and subject to accountable public supervision.

## Problem

Cost, coverage, language, device access, closed knowledge, unreliable connectivity, and dependence on a few platforms prevent many people from using digital knowledge and AI on fair terms.

## People

Households, learners, workers, libraries, schools, underserved communities, public agencies, and people in places with limited or unreliable internet.

## Root causes

- Concentrated infrastructure and platform gatekeepers can set access, price, and usage terms (`cluster-concentrated-gatekeepers`, D).
- Cross-border standards, funding, and shared knowledge infrastructure require cooperation that can stall (`cluster-weak-global-cooperation`, K).

## Tool ideas from the plan

Create an access observatory for connectivity cost and coverage, plus offline knowledge packs bundled with a private local AI tutor. Compare broadband rights, universal-service funds, subsidies, libraries, public networks, open models, and public compute, including what worked, lapsed, and why.

## Governance options

Compare rights-based, subsidy, municipal, cooperative, regulated-carrier, library, open-source, and mixed models on coverage, total cost, quality, privacy, accessibility, sustainability, and independence from political or commercial control.

## Guardrails

- Free access and government supervision are owner directions and contested policy choices; evidence must cover costs, service quality, privacy, censorship, surveillance, capture, and independence.
- Prefer local processing, data minimization, open formats, inspectable sources, and user control.
- Do not present local-model output as authoritative; preserve source links and uncertainty.
- Review content licences, model licences, update paths, and child-safety needs before distribution.
- Keep model weights and knowledge bases on user devices where possible so services cannot be remotely disabled or centrally altered.
- Never collect or transmit user query histories or telemetry.

## Evidence

The P9 brief documents global offline populations and affordability gaps, the end of the U.S. ACP subsidy, broadband-rights and public-rail precedents, and the surveillance risk of state supervision. It proposes offline knowledge and local AI as a privacy-preserving complement to public access infrastructure.

### Prior art

- [Kiwix](https://kiwix.org/en/applications/) (GPL-3.0) packages Wikipedia, Gutenberg, and medical wikis for offline use.
- [Ai2 OLMo](https://allenai.org/olmo) provides open weights, training code, and pre-training data under Apache 2.0.
- NYC Mesh and Guifi.net demonstrate community mesh networks; the [NAIRR pilot](https://www.nsf.gov/news/democratizing-future-ai-rd-nsf-launch-national-ai-research) provides public-benefit research compute.

### Options and trade-offs

- Commercial service with vouchers can deploy quickly, but prices, program expiry, and user tracking remain risks.
- A state-supervised subsidized utility can guarantee a baseline, but creates censorship and surveillance risks requiring technical and constitutional safeguards.
- Decentralized commons and local AI maximize privacy and independence, but require user hardware and offer less model scale.
- A public utility rail with encryption, open weights, and zero telemetry combines funding and privacy, but needs large capital investment and civil-liberties governance.
